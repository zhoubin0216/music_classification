\begin{abstract}
Recent foundation models for music understanding have demonstrated strong performance on a wide range of Music Information Retrieval (MIR) tasks. However, most existing benchmarks and pretraining corpora are heavily biased toward Western music traditions, making it unclear whether these models truly learn culturally generalizable music representations. In this work, we investigate cross-cultural music representation learning using MERT, a large-scale music foundation model, across both Western and Eastern musical genres. Since no unified benchmark currently exists for this task, we first construct a balanced cross-cultural dataset containing Western popular genres and multiple Eastern traditional and modern genres. We then perform a series of experiments including baseline classification, transfer learning, multi-task learning, confusion-aware hard mining, and layer-wise representation analysis. Our results show that MERT learns highly discriminative representations for traditional music genres, while modern genres from different cultures exhibit stronger overlap in latent space. Furthermore, direct transfer from Western music classifiers to Eastern genres produces significant negative transfer effects, suggesting that Western-centric representations do not generalize well to culturally distinct musical structures. Visualization analysis using UMAP and confusion matrices further reveals that traditional music retains strong cultural separability, whereas modern popular music exhibits increasing convergence across regions. These findings provide new insights into the cultural biases and representational properties of music foundation models.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Recent years have witnessed rapid progress in foundation models for music representation learning. Large-scale pretrained models such as MusicLM~\cite{agostinelli2023musiclm}, MuLan~\cite{huang2022mulan}, and MERT~\cite{li2024mert} have significantly improved performance across a wide range of Music Information Retrieval (MIR) tasks~\cite{humphrey2013feature, lee2009unsupervised, van2013deep}, including genre classification, tagging, retrieval, and music understanding. However, previous MIR research has primarily focused on Western datasets such as GTZAN~\cite{tzanetakis2002musical} and FMA~\cite{defferrard2016fma}, while cross-cultural music understanding remains relatively underexplored.

At the same time, globalization and industrialized music production have caused modern popular music genres from different regions to become increasingly similar in timbre and production style. In contrast, many traditional music genres still preserve strong regional and cultural uniqueness. This raises an interesting representation learning question: how do foundation music models organize traditional and modern music from different cultures in latent space?

To investigate this problem, we construct a unified cross-cultural music dataset containing both Western and Eastern genres, including traditional and modern music styles from China, Japan, Korea, India, and Western popular music. Based on representations extracted from MERT, we conduct a series of experiments including baseline genre classification, cross-cultural transfer learning, multi-task learning, confusion-aware hard mining, and layer-wise representation analysis.

Our experiments reveal several important findings. First, MERT already learns highly discriminative representations for many traditional music genres. Second, directly transferring Western-trained genre representations to Eastern music introduces strong negative transfer effects. Third, modern popular genres from different cultural regions exhibit significantly stronger overlap in latent space compared to traditional music genres. Finally, middle layers of MERT capture the most discriminative cultural information.

Our main contributions are summarized as follows:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
 \item We construct a unified cross-cultural music dataset containing both Western and Eastern music genres.

 \item We systematically investigate how MERT represents culturally distinct musical structures through classification, transfer learning, and layer-wise analysis.

 \item We show that traditional music genres remain highly separable in latent space, while modern popular music exhibits increasing cross-cultural convergence.
\end{itemize}

\section{Dataset Construction}
\subsection{Dataset Collecting}

To address this issue, we construct a cross-cultural music dataset by combining multiple data sources (Table~\ref{tab:western_genres} and Table~\ref{tab:eastern_genres}).

The Western subset is mainly derived from the GTZAN dataset~\cite{tzanetakis2002musical}, while the Carnatic genre is collected from a public Carnatic music dataset~\cite{krishnan2025sanidha}. The remaining Eastern genres are collected from Bilibili, where we manually selected high-quality and genre-consistent recordings. Only videos marked as reprintable or openly reusable were included to ensure ethical compliance for academic research. This design intentionally includes both culturally distinctive traditional genres and globally influenced modern genres.

\subsection{Preprocessing and Leakage-Aware Splitting}

All tracks are resampled to 24~kHz and segmented into 30-second audio slices~\cite{mcfee2015librosa} to increase training diversity. We use a 60\% / 20\% / 20\% train / validation / test split and adopt a strict two-level leakage protection.
First, slice-level leakage is prevented by splitting at the track level so that
no two slices from the same recording appear in different subsets. Second, \emph{artist-level leakage} is prevented through group-aware splitting: for the Western subset, we use the manually curated artist and song index released by
Sturm~\cite{sturm2013gtzan}\footnote{\url{https://github.com/boblsturm/GTZAN}} to assign each GTZAN file to an (artist, song) group, so that all recordings by the same artist---and any cross-genre song duplicates---are confined to a single split. For the Eastern subset, each recording is assigned a source identifier derived from its origin playlist or uploader; recordings sharing a source identifier are kept in the same split. All evaluation is performed using stratified group $k$-fold cross-validation (\texttt{StratifiedGroupKFold}, $k = 5$), which simultaneously balances class distribution and prevents group bleeding across folds.

We note that prior work routinely overlooks artist-level leakage on GTZAN, leading to inflated accuracies; Flexer~\cite{flexer2007closer} reports that introducing artist filters typically reduces reported accuracies by 5--15 percentage points. Our results below are reported under the leakage-safe protocol throughout.

\subsection{Feature Extraction with MERT}

We use MERT~\cite{li2024mert} as the backbone representation extractor as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:method}. Audio slices are processed through the pretrained transformer encoder, and hidden representations are extracted from multiple transformer layers.

For most experiments, we use pooled latent representations obtained from the final hidden states. We additionally perform layer-wise analysis by extracting representations from different transformer depths.

\subsection{Baseline Classifiers}
We first establish three baseline tasks: Western 10-class genre classification; Eastern 8-class genre classification; Unified 18-class cross-cultural classification.

A lightweight multilayer perceptron (MLP) classifier is trained on top of frozen MERT~\cite{li2024mert} representations. The classifier consists of fully connected layers with ReLU activations and dropout regularization.

\subsection{Transfer Learning Experiments}

To investigate cross-cultural transferability~\cite{pan2009survey, choi2017transfer}, we perform two transfer learning experiments.

First, we directly evaluate a Western-trained classifier on Eastern music samples without retraining. Second, we reuse the learned Western classifier while replacing the final 10-class prediction head with a new Eastern classification head.

\subsection{Multi-Task Learning}

We further investigate whether introducing external semantic knowledge improves cross-cultural classification. Besides the primary 18-class genre classification task, we introduce two auxiliary tasks~\cite{ruder2017overview}: traditional versus modern music classification and vocal versus instrumental classification. These auxiliary objectives encourage the latent representation to capture higher-level semantic organization beyond individual genres.

\subsection{Confusion-Aware Hard Mining}

Analysis of confusion matrices~\cite{susmaga2004confusion} reveals several consistently ambiguous genre pairs. To address these ambiguities, we introduce a lightweight confusion-aware hard mining strategy that increases the loss contribution of predefined difficult confusion directions.

Unlike symmetric metric learning objectives, our approach uses directional confusion penalties, reflecting the observation that genre confusion is often asymmetric.

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

All experiments are conducted using frozen MERT-v1-95M~\cite{li2024mert} representations with lightweight downstream classifiers implemented in PyTorch. Audio is processed at 24~kHz; for each 30-second slice we extract the hidden states from all 12 transformer layers and apply mean pooling over the time dimension, yielding one 768-dimensional vector per layer per slice. For the main classification experiments we use the final-layer representation; for the layer-wise probing experiment we use representations from each individual transformer layer.

We use \texttt{StratifiedGroupKFold} with $k=5$ (random seed $42$) so that each fold respects both class balance and the group constraints described in Section~2.2. Within each fold, training tracks are used for parameter fitting, validation tracks for early stopping, and test tracks for the final evaluation. We report the mean and standard deviation across the five test folds.

Track-level prediction is obtained by soft voting: slice-level posterior probabilities are averaged within a track and the maximum aggregated probability determines the final label. Because the dataset is moderately imbalanced, macro-F1 is used as the primary evaluation metric.

\subsection{Experimental Detail}

\paragraph{MLP classifier.}
The downstream classifier is a two-layer multilayer perceptron operating on
the 768-dimensional MERT representation. The architecture is
$\mathrm{Linear}(768 \!\to\! 64) \to \mathrm{ReLU} \to \mathrm{Dropout}(p\!=\!0.5)
\to \mathrm{Linear}(64 \!\to\! C)$, where $C$ is the number of target classes
($10$, $8$, or $18$ depending on the task). Aggregation modules, when used,
are inserted before the MLP: a learnable softmax-weighted sum over the 12
transformer-layer outputs (with $16$-dimensional projection head) for layer
aggregation, and a learnable attention pooling over chunks for chunk aggregation.

\paragraph{Training configuration.}
Models are optimized with AdamW (learning rate $3\times 10^{-4}$, weight
decay $1\times 10^{-3}$). We use cross-entropy loss with label smoothing
$0.05$ and apply class-weighted sampling proportional to inverse class
frequency to mitigate the imbalance among Eastern genres. Each model is
trained for up to $100$ epochs with early stopping on validation macro-F1
(patience $5$). Batch size is \todo{[fill in: e.g.\ 32 or 64]}.
After cross-validation, a final model is trained on the union of training
and validation tracks for the median best epoch across folds.

\paragraph{Transfer learning.}
The \emph{direct evaluation} setting feeds Eastern features through the
Western-trained MLP without any retraining; predictions are recorded as
projections onto the Western 10-class label space, and we report the
projection distribution, mean confidence, and predictive entropy (the
labels are non-overlapping, so accuracy is undefined). The
\emph{head replacement} setting freezes the hidden layer of the
Western-trained MLP and re-initializes only the final classification
layer, which is then trained on the Eastern training split with the
same optimizer and stopping criterion as the baseline.

\paragraph{Multi-task learning.}
The multi-task model shares the MLP hidden layer across three heads
operating on the unified 18-class data: the primary genre head, a binary
\emph{traditional vs.\ modern} head, and a binary \emph{vocal vs.\
instrumental} head. Auxiliary labels are deterministically assigned per
genre using a manually curated mapping (e.g.\ Guzheng/Guqin and Sitar are
labelled instrumental-traditional; K-pop and Western Pop are
vocal-modern). The total loss is
$\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{genre}} + \lambda_1\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{trad}}
+ \lambda_2\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{vocal}}$ with

\paragraph{Confusion-aware hard mining.}
Following an analysis of the baseline 18-class confusion matrix, we identify
asymmetric confusion pairs whose directional confusion rate exceeds
\todo{[fill in threshold, e.g.\ 0.10]}. For an anchor sample of true class
$a$ that is at risk of being confused with class $b$, we add a directional
penalty $\alpha \cdot p(b \mid x)$ to the standard cross-entropy loss, where
$\alpha = $ \todo{[fill in]}. The set of mined pairs includes
\emph{Enka $\to$ Sitar}, \emph{C-pop $\to$ Enka}, and \emph{Rock $\to$ Metal},

\paragraph{Layer-wise probing.}
To diagnose which transformer depth carries the most genre-discriminative
information, we train an independent linear probe on the pooled output of
each of the 12 transformer layers. The probe is a multinomial logistic
regression (\texttt{scikit-learn}, $C\!=\!1.0$, \texttt{class\_weight=balanced},
\texttt{lbfgs} solver, max $2000$ iterations); features are standardized using
statistics computed only on the training split of each fold. We deliberately
use a linear probe rather than the MLP head so that the reported numbers
reflect the linear separability of each layer's raw representation.


\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}

We report Accuracy, Macro-F1. Macro-F1 is used as the primary evaluation metric because it better reflects performance across both majority and minority genres.

To better understand the latent geometry learned by MERT~\cite{li2024mert}, we visualize pooled representations using UMAP dimensionality reduction~\cite{mcinnes2018umap}. We additionally analyze normalized confusion matrices to investigate genre-level ambiguities and cultural overlap.

\subsection{Negative Transfer Effects}

Both transfer learning experiments~\cite{pan2009survey, choi2017transfer} demonstrate clear negative transfer behavior.

Directly applying Western-trained classifiers to Eastern music produces severe performance degradation.

Furthermore, reusing Western-learned feature structures while replacing only the classification head also fails to improve Eastern classification, with the Macro-F1 score dropping significantly from 83.66\% to 52.22\%. This result suggests that representations learned from Western genres introduce strong negative transfer effects when applied to Eastern music understanding.

These findings suggest that representations optimized for Western genre boundaries do not transfer effectively to culturally distinct Eastern music structures.

As shown in Table~\ref{tab:ablation}, both multi-task learning~\cite{ruder2017overview} and confusion-aware hard mining~\cite{susmaga2004confusion} improve performance over the baseline model. Multi-task learning achieves the largest gain, improving test Macro-F1 from 0.7818 to 0.8189, while hard mining also provides a noticeable improvement.

However, combining both approaches does not lead to further gains. One possible explanation is that the two methods provide overlapping supervision signals. The confusion-aware hard mining strategy explicitly emphasizes ambiguous genre pairs such as Enka–Sitar and C-pop–Enka, which partially correspond to higher-level semantic attributes introduced by the auxiliary tasks (e.g., traditional versus modern music and vocal versus instrumental music). Therefore, both approaches may encourage the model to learn similar latent separations, leading to limited complementary effects.

Figure~\ref{fig:layerwise} reveals two qualitatively different
representations across our two label spaces. For Western genres, the
discriminative information rises through the early-to-middle layers
and peaks at layer~7, then degrades by about 10 macro-F1 points by
layer~12. This pattern matches prior findings that the deepest
transformer layers of self-supervised audio models become specialized
toward their masked acoustic reconstruction objective and are no
longer optimal for downstream genre classification.

The Eastern curve tells a different story. Layer~1 alone---the
shallowest transformer output, which is closest to raw acoustic
features---already separates the 8 Eastern classes with macro-F1
$\approx 0.86$, and the peak at layer~5 only adds about 3 points
on top. We interpret this as evidence that the Eastern classes in
our dataset are largely distinguishable by low-level timbral and
spectral properties (instrument type, vocal vs.\ instrumental,
production quality) rather than by the deeper rhythmic, harmonic, or
stylistic abstractions that MERT's middle layers encode. In other
words, MERT's hierarchical representation is being heavily exercised
when classifying Western genres but is largely redundant for our
Eastern label space.

This asymmetry has two consequences for interpreting the rest of our
results. First, the strong baseline performance on Eastern 8-class
(Section~5.1) should not be read as ``MERT understands Eastern music
well''; it is consistent with the Eastern classes being acoustically
distant from each other for reasons that any shallow audio model
could exploit. Second, the negative-transfer findings of Section~5.2
should be understood as MERT's mid-to-deep layers being shaped by
Western musical structure: when these layers are reused, they remain
useful for Western classification but fail to provide useful
inductive bias for Eastern classification.

\section{Discussion}

Our experiments suggest that current music foundation models do not learn fully culture-invariant music representations.

Instead, MERT~\cite{li2024mert} appears to capture a combination of universal acoustic patterns and culturally specific structural priors. Traditional music genres remain highly separable due to distinctive instrumentation, tonal systems, and vocal techniques. In contrast, modern popular genres from different regions exhibit substantially stronger overlap in latent space, likely because globalization and industrial music production have increasingly standardized timbral characteristics and production styles.

The observed negative transfer~\cite{pan2009survey, choi2017transfer} effects further indicate that representations optimized for Western genre structures do not naturally generalize to culturally distinct Eastern music understanding. This suggests that existing large-scale music pretraining corpora may implicitly encode Western-centric structural biases.

Interestingly, lightweight improvements such as multi-task learning~\cite{ruder2017overview} and confusion-aware hard mining~\cite{susmaga2004confusion} provide only incremental gains. Although both approaches improve performance individually, their combined effect remains limited, suggesting that the primary challenge may not be classifier design itself but rather intrinsic representational overlap among culturally related music genres.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, despite combining multiple sources, the constructed dataset remains relatively small compared with large-scale music benchmarks and contains moderate class imbalance for some genres. Second, the current Eastern subset only covers a limited number of cultural regions and musical traditions. Many culturally diverse music systems from Africa, the Middle East, Southeast Asia, and other regions are not represented. Finally, our experiments rely on frozen MERT representations and lightweight downstream classifiers, meaning that the adaptation ability of fully fine-tuned foundation models remains unexplored.

Future work may investigate larger and more culturally balanced datasets, culturally adaptive pretraining strategies, and self-supervised objectives specifically designed for cross-cultural music understanding. In addition, incorporating music-theoretic knowledge such as rhythm structures, scales, instrument taxonomy, and cultural metadata may further improve the interpretability and generalization ability of future music foundation models.

\section{Conclusion}

In this work, we investigated cross-cultural music representation learning using MERT across both Western and Eastern genres. We constructed a unified cross-cultural music dataset containing traditional and modern genres from multiple cultural regions and conducted a series of experiments including baseline classification, transfer learning, multi-task learning, hard mining, layer-wise analysis, and latent space visualization.

Our experiments reveal three major findings: (1) traditional music genres remain highly separable in latent space, (2) modern popular genres exhibit substantially stronger cross-cultural overlap, and (3) Western-trained representations introduce significant negative transfer effects when applied to Eastern music understanding.

Overall, these findings suggest that current music foundation models still contain meaningful cultural biases despite strong representation capabilities, highlighting the importance of culturally diverse training corpora and evaluation benchmarks for future music understanding systems.
